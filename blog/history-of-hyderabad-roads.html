A history of Hyderabad's roads: from the Charminar to the Outer Ring Road

The history of Hyderabad roads: the Qutb Shahi city, Tank Bund, Nizam-era planning, Secunderabad, numbered Banjara Hills roads, HITEC City, the ORR and metro.

The history of Hyderabad's roads is a story of a city that kept adding new layers rather than replacing old ones. The narrow streets round the Charminar date from the 1590s; the road along Tank Bund sits on a sixteenth-century dam; Banjara Hills and Jubilee Hills were laid out on rock in the twentieth century; and HITEC City, the Outer Ring Road and the metro have pushed the city far to the west and beyond.

Knowing where each layer came from explains a lot about how the roads drive today: why the Old City is a maze of one-way lanes, why Tank Bund is so straight, and why your address in Jubilee Hills is a number.

The Qutb Shahi city around the Charminar

Before Hyderabad there was Golconda, the fortified capital of the Qutb Shahi kings on its granite hill to the west. By the late sixteenth century the fort was crowded, and in 1591 Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah founded a new city on the south bank of the river Musi. The Charminar, built that year, stands at its centre.

The new city was planned around the monument, with main streets running out from it towards the points of the compass and markets along them. That is why so many Old City roads still meet at the Charminar, and why the streets around it feel like spokes of a wheel. Over four centuries the gaps filled in with houses, shops and lanes that were never meant for motor traffic. Today many are one-way, and the circle round the monument works like a crowded roundabout.

Our Charminar Old City driving guide covers what those streets are like to drive now.

Hussain Sagar and Tank Bund

Even before the city itself, the Qutb Shahis were shaping its geography. Hussain Sagar was created in the 1560s (most sources say 1562) during the reign of Ibrahim Quli Qutb Shah, by damming a tributary of the Musi. It is named after Hussain Shah Wali, who oversaw the work. For centuries the lake supplied drinking water to the city.

The embankment on its eastern side, the Tank Bund, became a natural road: a dry, level line across low ground between what grew into Hyderabad to the south and Secunderabad to the north. It was widened in 1946, and in the 1980s a row of statues of figures from Telugu history and culture was added along it. Necklace Road, which curves round the western and northern shores, came much later.

That history is why Tank Bund Road is so straight and so exposed to the lake. See our Tank Bund Road driving guide for how it drives today.

The Nizams, Secunderabad and a planned city

Asaf Jahi rule and the cantonment

After the Mughal conquest of Golconda in 1687, Hyderabad passed in the eighteenth century to the Asaf Jahi dynasty, the Nizams. British influence grew, and in the early nineteenth century a large British military cantonment was set up on the land north of Hussain Sagar. It was named Secunderabad after Sikandar Jah, the third Nizam; most accounts date it to 1806.

Secunderabad grew as a separate town with its own grid of parade grounds, barracks, bazaars and, later, the railway. That is the origin of the "twin cities", and of the way traffic still funnels between them across a few links, Tank Bund among them.

After the 1908 flood

On 28 September 1908 the Musi flooded catastrophically, killing thousands and destroying large parts of the city along its banks. The disaster changed how Hyderabad was planned. In 1912, under the seventh Nizam, Mir Osman Ali Khan, a City Improvement Board was set up to rebuild and plan the city. Engineer M. Visvesvaraya advised on flood protection, which led to the Osman Sagar and Himayat Sagar reservoirs upstream.

The years that followed brought many of the grand public buildings beside the Musi, wider roads, and new planned neighbourhoods beyond the old walls. The city began to spread north and west, towards the rocky hills that would become Banjara Hills.

The numbered roads of Banjara Hills and Jubilee Hills

West of Hussain Sagar the land rises into granite hills and boulders. As the city grew in the twentieth century, these hills were laid out as residential areas with wide plots, winding roads following the contours, and a plain naming scheme: the roads were simply numbered.

Banjara Hills came first. Jubilee Hills, further west, was developed largely from the 1960s through a cooperative housing society. In both, the numbers stuck. People still give directions as "Road No. 1", "Road No. 36" or "Road No. 45", and the roads still twist and climb the way the rock dictated. Banjara Hills Road No. 1 climbs steadily from Masab Tank, a legacy of the terrain rather than any plan.

HITEC City, flyovers, the ORR and the metro

Cyberabad

Hyderabad became the capital of Andhra Pradesh in 1956, and of Telangana in 2014. Its biggest change of direction came at the end of the 1990s. Cyber Towers, the first landmark building of HITEC City in Madhapur, was inaugurated on 22 November 1998. The IT district that grew around it, often called Cyberabad, spread through Madhapur, Gachibowli, Kondapur and the Financial District.

The roads there are a different generation: wide, divided corridors built for office traffic, with big junctions and, more recently, the cable-stayed bridge over Durgam Cheruvu. Our HITEC City to Jubilee Hills drive follows one of these corridors from Cyber Towers back into the numbered roads of Jubilee Hills.

Flyovers and the elevated expressway

As traffic grew, the city went up. Flyovers were built over the busiest junctions, and on 19 October 2009 the P. V. Narasimha Rao Expressway, an elevated road of about 11.6 km from Mehdipatnam to Aramghar, opened to traffic towards the new airport at Shamshabad. Many more flyovers and underpasses have followed. Our guide to driving on flyovers in Hyderabad covers what they demand of a driver.

The Outer Ring Road

The Outer Ring Road, an access-controlled expressway of about 158 km, loops right round the city. It was built in stages from the late 2000s and completed as a full ring by the end of the 2010s. It lets traffic between the highways bypass the centre and has drawn new development, from Gachibowli to Shamshabad, along its length.

Hyderabad Metro

The first stretch of Hyderabad Metro, about 30 km from Miyapur to Nagole, was inaugurated on 28 November 2017. Much of it runs on viaducts above the middle of main roads, which changed the look of many streets and left pillars in the median that drivers now steer round every day.

Where Hyderabad's roads are heading

The pattern of adding layers continues. Further metro extensions have been planned, more flyovers and underpasses are being built at congested junctions, and a Regional Ring Road much further out has been proposed to take long-distance traffic round the wider region. Timelines for projects like these change, so treat any date you read as a plan rather than a promise.

What is unlikely to change is the city's character: a four-hundred-year-old core, a lakeside link between the twin cities, numbered hill roads and fast new corridors, all within a short drive of each other.

Frequently asked questions

How old is Hyderabad?

Hyderabad was founded in 1591 by Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah, the fifth Qutb Shahi ruler, who moved his capital out from Golconda. The Charminar, built the same year, marks the centre of the new city.

Why is it called Tank Bund?

Tank Bund is the embankment, or bund, that holds back Hussain Sagar, the lake (tank) built in the 1560s under Ibrahim Quli Qutb Shah. The road along its top became the main link between Hyderabad and Secunderabad.

Why are Banjara Hills and Jubilee Hills roads numbered?

Both areas were laid out as planned residential layouts on rocky hills, and the roads were simply given numbers rather than names. The numbers stuck, so addresses there still read Road No. 1, Road No. 36 and so on.

When did Hyderabad Metro open?

The first stretch, about 30 km from Miyapur to Nagole, was inaugurated on 28 November 2017 and opened to the public the next day. More sections followed in later years.
