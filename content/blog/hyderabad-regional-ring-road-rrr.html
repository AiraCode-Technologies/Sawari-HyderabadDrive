The Hyderabad Regional Ring Road (RRR) is a planned second ring road around the city, far outside the Outer Ring Road. As of November 2026 it is still mostly a plan: the northern part has its land and is waiting to start construction, while the southern part's route has not yet been approved.

This is a fast-moving project. Everything below is as of November 2026, from official statements and news reports at the time of writing. Dates have slipped many times, so treat any target year as a hope rather than a promise.

What the Hyderabad Regional Ring Road is

The Outer Ring Road now sits inside the built-up city in many places. The RRR is meant to be the next ring out: a high-speed road that links the district towns around Hyderabad, connects every national highway leaving the city, and lets long-distance traffic and freight avoid the metropolitan area entirely.

It is split into two halves, which are being handled very differently:

  • The northern part, from Sangareddy round the north of the city to Choutuppal, about 161.5 km.
  • The southern part, from Choutuppal round the south back to Sangareddy, reported at roughly 182 to 201 km depending on the alignment.

Together that is a ring of around 340 km or more, passing through parts of Sangareddy, Medak, Siddipet, Yadadri Bhuvanagiri, Nalgonda, Rangareddy, Vikarabad and neighbouring districts.

The northern part: land done, construction waiting

The northern part is the one that is closest to becoming real. It has been approved by the Union Ministry of Road Transport and Highways as national highway NH 161AA under the Bharatmala Pariyojana programme, and it will be built by the National Highways Authority of India (NHAI).

Route and design

The northern alignment runs through or near Sangareddy, Narsapur, Toopran, Gajwel, Jagdevpur, Bhongir and Choutuppal. It is planned as a six-lane road. In July 2026 the Union road transport minister, Nitin Gadkari, gave its length as 161.518 km and its estimated cost as about Rs 23,936 crore, to be built under the hybrid annuity model, where the government pays the builder in instalments over time.

Earlier reports said the design had been changed from a fully access-controlled expressway to a national highway with a lower design speed. The final details will only be clear once the road is built, so it is fair to say it will be a fast, six-lane highway rather than to promise a particular speed.

Land and tenders

Land acquisition, often the slowest step for any Indian highway, is largely done. The minister's July 2026 statement said acquisition notices had been issued for about 99 per cent of the land needed. In October 2026 the Telangana Roads and Buildings minister said land acquisition for the northern part was complete, while another report that month put it at over 90 per cent. The state pays half the land cost, and the Centre pays the rest and the full construction cost.

The tenders have been the sticking point. NHAI invited bids for the northern part in late 2025, then extended the deadline again and again during 2026. At the time of writing, reports say the extensions are because the Union Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs has not yet given the final clearance needed to start the work. No construction contract had been reported as awarded as of November 2026.

The southern part: still finding its route

The southern part is at a much earlier stage. Its job is to complete the ring through the southern districts and connect the RRR with the new developments south of the city, including the planned Bharat Future City, Pharma City and industrial parks.

  • The state cabinet approved alignment options in mid-2025, and a revised alignment was sent to the Centre in February 2026.
  • NHAI appointed a consultant for the detailed project report, which was still being prepared in 2026.
  • In October 2026 the state handed the southern part to the Hyderabad Growth Corridor Limited (HGCL), the HMDA company that already works on the ORR and on land for Future City.
  • The alignment has faced protests from farmers and landowners along the proposed route.

Reports describe it as a proposed six-lane road of about 201 km passing through areas such as Choutuppal, Amangal and Sangareddy. As of November 2026 the southern alignment had not been approved by the Centre, so there is no final route, cost or timetable to report.

What the RRR will mean for drivers

When it opens, the RRR should change journeys in a few clear ways.

  • Freight round, not through. Lorries travelling between, say, the Mumbai highway and the Vijayawada highway could stay on the RRR instead of using the ORR. That should ease the heavy-vehicle lanes on the ORR.
  • Faster links between district towns. Places like Sangareddy, Gajwel and Bhongir would be connected to each other directly, without going via Hyderabad.
  • New growth along the ring. As the ORR did for Gachibowli and Kokapet, the RRR is expected to draw logistics parks, industry and housing to its interchanges.
  • Radial roads. Plans link the RRR to the ORR and the city through a set of radial roads, some of which are being built or planned separately.

For drivers, the habits needed will be the same as on the ORR: lane discipline, long following gaps, early lane changes for exits and care around heavy vehicles. A new highway through farmland also means slow tractors, livestock and people crossing near villages in the early years, so it pays to stay alert even when the road is empty.

Sawari and the RRR

Sawari does not include the RRR, which does not exist yet, or the ORR. It is a free browser driving simulator on five real city routes in Hyderabad, built from OpenStreetMap. What it is good for is building the basics that make highway driving safe: steady lane keeping, signalling every lane change and reading traffic ahead.
